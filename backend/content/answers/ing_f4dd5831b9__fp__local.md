**Why stronger models can still fail**  
At its core, an AI agent is a *policy* that maps observations \(o_t\) to actions \(a_t\). A larger model improves the *expressive capacity* of this mapping, but it does not alter two fundamental constraints:

1. **Data distribution mismatch** – The policy learns from trajectories generated by past agents (or humans). If those trajectories never contain a particular corner case, no amount of weight scaling can produce correct behaviour there; the loss surface is flat for that region.  
2. **Reward sparsity & bias** – Even with perfect perception, the agent’s objective may be ill‑posed. A dense reward that encourages speed may implicitly reward reckless manoeuvres. The model will learn a *local optimum* that maximises the surrogate objective but violates safety constraints.

Thus, larger models merely sharpen the *approximation* of an already mis‑specified optimisation problem; they do not correct its specification errors.

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
The real bottleneck is *distribution shift in the policy’s own environment*. When a model improves, it explores new states that were unseen during training. The agent then faces “unknown unknowns” where its policy has no empirical grounding. Mitigating this requires *online curriculum learning* or *formal verification* of safety invariants—techniques that constrain exploration rather than just increase capacity.

In short, stronger models don’t cure failures because the failures stem from misaligned objectives and unseen states, not from insufficient representational power.
