Think of a bank account as a big, shared ledger in a city’s town hall. Everyone who lives there can write their own “I owe you” notes on the board, but only the town clerk (the bank) can read, approve, or change them. If you want to pay someone, you hand your note to the clerk, who then updates the board and gives you a receipt.

A blockchain wallet is like a personal notebook that is also copied to every person in the neighborhood. When you write a new “I owe you” note, it instantly appears on all those copies. No single clerk can erase or alter it—everyone’s copy must agree before the note stays. Thus, your money moves directly between people without a middle‑man, but everyone still sees and verifies each move.

So: banks keep a central ledger; blockchains keep a distributed one where every participant validates changes.
