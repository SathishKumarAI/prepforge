**Why a single ML model can’t simply “fly” from one city to another**

When you train a predictive system in City A it learns the joint law  
\(p_{A}(x,y)\) of inputs \(x\) (e.g., traffic patterns, weather, user behavior) and outputs \(y\) (e.g., demand, prices).  
If you naively ship that model to City B, you implicitly assume  

\[
p_{A}(x,y)=p_{B}(x,y),
\]

i.e. the same conditional distribution holds everywhere. In reality, even subtle cultural or infrastructural differences change the *support* of \(x\) and shift the mapping from \(x\) to \(y\). A model that minimizes expected loss under one distribution will incur a higher risk under another—a classic **covariate shift** problem.

The deeper principle is that ML models are *estimates of the optimal decision rule* for a particular probability law. If that law changes, so does the optimal rule. Therefore, deployment across cities requires *domain adaptation*: re‑estimating or correcting \(p_{B}(x,y)\) using local data, or learning a *shared representation* that is invariant to city‑specific factors (e.g., through adversarial training).  

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
It’s not enough to collect more data from City B; you must actively **align the latent feature space** so that the model’s internal notion of “typical” input matches each city’s reality. A small, well‑designed *adaptation layer* (e.g., a linear transformation learned on a handful of local samples) can dramatically reduce deployment risk, far more than scaling up the base network or adding noise to inputs. This aligns with information‑theoretic ideas: you’re minimizing the KL divergence between the adapted distribution and the true city‑specific one with minimal extra data.
