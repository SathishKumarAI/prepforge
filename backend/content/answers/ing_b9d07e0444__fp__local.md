### From the Problem to Proof

When a customer asks *“Did your model write this document?”* we face a classic **identification** problem: given an output \(y\) and a set of candidate generators \(\{M_1,\dots,M_k\}\), decide which \(M_i\) produced it.  
The only reliable evidence comes from the **information‑theoretic fingerprints** that a generator inevitably leaves:

| Evidence | Why it works | What we can prove |
|----------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Model metadata** (architecture, weights hash, training corpus hash) | The model’s state is a fixed point in parameter space. | “The document was generated by *exactly* the instance with SHA‑256 = ….” |
| **Deterministic sampling trace** (seed, temperature schedule, top‑k list) | Sampling is a stochastic process; the path through the probability distribution is unique for a given seed. | “With seed = 42, the model chose token t₁→t₂→…→t_n.” |
| **Token‑level log** (log‑probabilities per step) | The likelihood of a sequence under a given LM is computable; mismatches imply different models or settings. | “The log‑probability of this sentence under \(M_i\) differs by > 5 bits from that under \(M_j\).” |

### Non‑obvious Insight

A **single token’s conditional probability** can already rule out many competitors, because the entropy of modern LMs is so low on high‑frequency tokens. Thus, even a short excerpt often suffices to disambiguate models—something most people overlook when they assume long passages are required.

### What You Deliver

- A cryptographic proof that *this* model instance (with its exact weights and training hash) produced the text.
- Optionally, a deterministic sampling trace if you control the seed.
- Statistical evidence (log‑probabilities) showing that no other candidate model could plausibly generate the same sequence under identical settings.

These are the only things that can be *rigorously* proven; anything beyond—such as “human‑written” versus “machine‑written”—remains a hypothesis, not a certifiable fact.
