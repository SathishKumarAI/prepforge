**Why deep learning needs a solid grasp of the math behind it**

At its core, training a neural network is an *optimization* problem: we seek parameters θ that minimise a loss function \(L(\theta)\).  
Because \(L\) is typically non‑convex and high‑dimensional, we rely on stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to traverse the loss landscape. The success of SGD hinges on three intertwined principles:

1. **Gradient geometry** – The update \(\Delta\theta = -\eta\nabla L(\theta)\) moves along the steepest descent direction. Understanding how gradients behave (e.g., exploding/vanishing gradients in recurrent nets) explains why certain architectures (LSTM, residual blocks) were invented.

2. **Probabilistic regularisation** – Adding weight decay or dropout is equivalent to placing a prior over θ and performing MAP estimation. Recognising this Bayesian view clarifies that “regularisation” isn’t merely a trick; it encodes our uncertainty about the model’s capacity.

3. **Information flow** – The *information bottleneck* principle states that a hidden layer should compress input \(X\) while preserving predictive information about target \(Y\). This gives an objective‑free justification for depth: each layer progressively trades off compression against relevance, leading to hierarchical representations.

A non‑obvious insight most practitioners miss is that **batch normalisation’s empirical success can be seen as a variance‑control mechanism on the *signal propagation* rather than just a stabiliser of gradients**. By keeping activations with unit mean and variance, BN implicitly ensures that the Jacobian of each layer remains close to identity, which keeps the loss landscape well‑conditioned across depth.

In short, without an understanding of geometry, probability, and information theory, you’ll treat deep learning as a black‑box hyperparameter search. With those principles in hand, you can design architectures, choose optimisers, and debug failures with principled intuition.
