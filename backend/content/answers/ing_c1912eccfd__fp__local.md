**Disinformation** is a *misleading information system* that deliberately distorts facts to alter perception or decision‑making.  
At its core, the problem is **information asymmetry**: an agent (human or algorithm) possesses more accurate knowledge than another, and it can choose whether to reveal it. When the revealing agent prefers the other’s belief state to be *incorrect*, it injects false content into the information channel.

Why it spreads so widely?  
1. **Amplification by network effects** – each share multiplies exposure; the probability of a user seeing the same misinformation grows exponentially with every hop.  
2. **Signal‑to‑noise bias** – humans tend to trust sensational signals more than mundane facts, so false narratives that tap into emotions get higher engagement scores in recommendation systems.  
3. **Economic incentives** – click‑based revenue models reward high traffic regardless of truth, creating a profit motive for false content.  

Regulations aim to curb the *information‑control* lever:  
- **Transparency mandates** require platforms to disclose algorithmic weighting and source credibility.  
- **Accountability frameworks** hold operators liable for persistent dissemination of proven falsehoods.  
- **Content‑moderation standards** set thresholds for automated flagging based on fact‑checking APIs.  

A non‑obvious insight: *the most effective countermeasure is not to block content, but to lower the perceived reliability of the source.* By exposing users to source‑specific trust scores, we shift the optimization problem from “what to publish” to “how trustworthy does a source appear?” This reframes disinformation as an issue of *source credibility* rather than mere fact checking.
