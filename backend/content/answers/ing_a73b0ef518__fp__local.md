**Why “pass‑at‑k → pass‑at‑1” is a fallacy in reinforcement learning**

At its core, reinforcement learning (RL) optimises an *expected return* over trajectories generated by a policy π. The objective is  

\[
J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t} \gamma^{t} r_t\right].
\]

A “pass‑at‑k” metric simply asks whether *any* of the top‑k sampled trajectories achieves a target reward. Formally,

\[
\text{Pass@k}(\pi)=\mathbb{P}\bigl[\max_{i=1}^{k} R_i \geq T\bigr],
\]

where \(R_i\) is the return of the i‑th sample and \(T\) the threshold. This is a *probability* over a *maximum*, not an expectation.

Because RL optimises the mean return, it can increase \(\mathbb{E}[R]\) by making the policy more conservative: a single high‑reward trajectory with very low variance may dominate Pass@k while keeping the overall expectation modest. Conversely, a highly variable policy might achieve Pass@1 (a lucky roll) but yield a lower expected return and thus be disfavoured.

The deeper principle is that **maximisation of expectations does not imply maximisation of tail probabilities**—an instance of Jensen’s inequality in reverse:  

\[
\mathbb{E}[\max_i R_i] \geq \max_i \mathbb{E}[R_i].
\]

Hence, RL training that focuses on mean performance can *under‑exploit* the high‑reward tail. One non‑obvious insight: **optimising a surrogate loss that directly penalises low‑rank failures (e.g., using rank‑based rewards or percentile‑threshold objectives) is required to push Pass@k toward Pass@1**; otherwise, the policy will settle for “good enough” averages rather than “perfect on the first try.”
