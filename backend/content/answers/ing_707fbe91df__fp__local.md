**Why blockers arise**

Adopting an AI system is a *joint‑optimization* problem: the organization must simultaneously **maximize value** (speed, accuracy, new revenue) and **minimize cost** (training time, data curation, risk). Blockers surface when one part of this trade‑off is ignored—e.g., if the model delivers high accuracy but requires a 6‑month data pipeline overhaul, employees perceive it as *expensive* rather than valuable.

**Why a systematic approach works**

1. **Map the value‑cost curve** for each stakeholder (data scientists, product managers, executives).  
2. **Identify friction points** where marginal cost exceeds marginal benefit—these are the blockers.  
3. **Re‑architect in stages**: start with low‑friction “quick wins” that shift the curve leftward, then tackle higher‑cost levers once momentum builds.

This mirrors *convex optimization*: small, incremental moves toward a global optimum (high adoption) avoid local minima (resistance).

**One non‑obvious insight**

People resist because they fear *knowledge loss*—they worry their expertise will be replaced. Address this by **co‑creating the model**: let domain experts annotate training data and validate predictions. This turns AI from a black box into an *augmented skill set*, shifting the cost from “replacement” to “skill expansion.” When stakeholders see themselves as co‑authors rather than competitors, blockers evaporate.

In short, treat adoption like a multi‑objective optimization problem, identify friction points via value–cost mapping, and transform perceived threats into collaborative opportunities.
