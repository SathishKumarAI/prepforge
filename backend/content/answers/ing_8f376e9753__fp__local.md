**Why 2026 AI pay scales look the way they do**

The *compensation benchmark* is the solution to a simple optimisation problem:  
maximize firm value while attracting enough talent to solve hard technical problems, subject to budget constraints and the market’s opportunity cost of labour.

1. **Talent‑value trade‑off** – Every AI role can be mapped to a *utility function* \(U_i = \alpha_i \times R_i\), where \(\alpha_i\) captures skill scarcity (e.g., deep RL, federated learning) and \(R_i\) the revenue impact of an average hire. The benchmark sets salary \(S_i\) so that \(S_i \approx U_i + \text{cost of capital}\).  
2. **Budget‑efficiency** – Firms internalise a *shadow price* for each dollar spent on talent: the expected marginal increase in net present value (NPV) per unit salary. Benchmarking equates this shadow price across roles, preventing over‑spending on low‑impact positions and under‑paying high‑impact ones.

This is an instance of the *matching theorem* in labour economics: wages adjust until supply equals demand at equilibrium.
