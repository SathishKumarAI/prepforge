**Why the copyright line appears in an RFC**

An RFC is *software‑free* only insofar as its text can be copied, but it is not a public‑domain document.  
The RFC Editor (IETF) must protect its authors’ intellectual property while still allowing broad reuse of the specification. The solution is to embed a **copyright notice** that:

1. **States ownership** – “Copyright © 2024 IETF Trust” tells readers who holds legal rights.  
2. **Defines the license** – The following clause (the *IETF Trust’s RFC License*) grants everyone a perpetual, worldwide, royalty‑free right to use, modify, and redistribute the RFC text **only for the purpose of implementing or describing the standard**.  
3. **Preserves legal certainty** – By making the license explicit in the document itself, it avoids later ambiguity that could arise from relying on external terms.

The notice is thus a *minimal contract* embedded in the specification: it ensures that anyone who copies the RFC does so under clear, predictable terms and that the IETF can still enforce its rights if the text is used outside the intended scope. This balances openness (essential for standards development) with protection of authors’ intellectual property—an elegant compromise rooted in both copyright law and the collaborative ethos of open‑standards engineering.
