**Anomaly = “hard‑to‑isolate” point**

In a dataset every observation is generated by some underlying process.  
Typical points lie in dense regions; an *anomaly* is one that would require
an extreme amount of conditioning to be described—its probability density is
negligible relative to the bulk.

Isolation Trees formalise this intuition via *recursive partitioning*.  
At each node a random feature and threshold split the space.  
The key observation: **the expected depth of a point equals the number of
random cuts needed before it becomes isolated**.  
For normal points, many cuts are required (they share volume with neighbours),
so their average depth grows logarithmically with sample size \(n\).  
Anomalies lie in sparse pockets; a single random cut often isolates them,
yielding shallow depths.

Thus the anomaly score is simply the average tree depth over an ensemble of
random splits. No density estimation, no distance metric—only combinatorial
counting of cuts.  

*Non‑obvious insight:*  
The method exploits **information entropy** implicitly: each random split
provides at most one bit of information about a point’s location; anomalies
consume fewer bits because they occupy less volume.  Consequently, Isolation
Trees are both computationally light and robust to high dimensionality,
since the probability that a random cut separates an anomaly is high even when
the ambient space is huge.
