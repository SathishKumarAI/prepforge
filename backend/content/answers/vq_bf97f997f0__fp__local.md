The core problem a sport solves is *optimal allocation of effort to achieve mastery*.  
For an individual athlete, the cost–benefit calculus is straightforward: effort → personal skill gain, with no external constraints. The optimal strategy is to maximize the marginal utility of each training unit—hence the appeal of solo disciplines where feedback loops are immediate and control is total.

In a team setting, the problem becomes one of *coordination economics*. Each member’s payoff now depends on others’ actions; the Nash equilibrium often requires sacrificing individual maximal effort for collective benefit. The optimal strategy shifts from personal skill maximization to optimizing *joint utility* under uncertainty (e.g., teammate reliability). This introduces a richer information‑theoretic structure: teammates must exchange signals, and the team’s performance is bounded by the weakest link.

Thus, the preference isn’t merely about skill development but about aligning with one of two fundamental motivational architectures—self‑efficacy versus social efficacy.
