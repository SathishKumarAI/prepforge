**Why salaries vary by role and geography**

At its core, a salary is the price paid for *expected future value* that an employee brings to a firm.  
For an AI/ML engineer, that value is two‑fold:  

1. **Technical productivity** – the ability to turn data into models that generate revenue or cut costs.  
2. **Risk mitigation** – expertise in bias, privacy, and regulatory compliance that prevents costly penalties.

In 2026, these factors are weighted differently across roles:

**Geographic multipliers**

Salaries rise where the *opportunity cost* (living expenses + talent scarcity) is high and where *demand* (AI‑heavy industries) is concentrated. Silicon Valley’s premium (~+30%) reflects both higher costs of living and a dense cluster of venture capital, while emerging hubs like Austin or Berlin offer lower salaries but lower living costs and a growing talent pool.

**Deeper principle: opportunity cost + information asymmetry**

The market price for an AI engineer is the product of *expected marginal revenue* (the incremental profit from their work) and *opportunity cost*. In regions where firms must compete globally, information asymmetry—where employers cannot perfectly gauge skill quality—leads to a premium on proven expertise. Hence, senior researchers in high‑cost cities command the highest salaries.

**Non‑obvious insight**

Most people think salary is purely about technical skill, but *regulatory risk management* has become a hidden premium. In 2026, firms that embed privacy‑by‑design and explainability into their pipelines can avoid fines worth millions; engineers who know how to build such systems are paid at the top end of every role, regardless of location.
