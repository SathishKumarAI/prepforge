**Why we care about likes, comments and shares**

At its core an Instagram‑style feed is a *recommendation* problem: for each user we must surface the most relevant posts in real time. The three “engagement” signals—likes, comments, shares—are the observable outcomes that tell us which content is valuable. They are not arbitrary counters; they are *feedback variables* that shape future recommendations and drive growth.

**Designing the counter service**

1. **Write‑heavy but read‑light**  
   Every tap must be persisted with minimal latency. A write‑skewed architecture (e.g., a sharded key‑value store keyed by `post_id`) guarantees atomic increments while keeping contention low.

2. **Eventual consistency vs strong consistency**  
   For likes/comments we can tolerate eventual consistency: the user sees their action immediately, but the global count may lag by milliseconds—acceptable because the impact on recommendation is minimal. Shares are rarer; a stronger guarantee (e.g., transactional write to both counter and share‑log) ensures accurate propagation.

3. **Batching & compaction**  
   In production we batch increments into memcached or Redis, flush them every 100 ms, then persist to a columnar store for analytics. Compaction reduces hot spot traffic and keeps the counter service lightweight.

4. **Backpressure & rate‑limiting**  
   A single user can’t spam likes; a token bucket per user prevents abuse while still allowing legitimate bursts.

**Non‑obvious insight**

The *entropy* of a post’s engagement distribution is a better predictor of future popularity than raw counts. By maintaining the histogram of likes/comments over time (e.g., via a lightweight sketch), we can feed a reinforcement‑learning model that rewards posts whose engagement rate is increasing, not just high absolute numbers. This captures viral momentum early and mitigates the “rich‑get‑richer” bias that simple counters introduce.
