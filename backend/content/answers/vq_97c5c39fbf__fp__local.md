**Deriving “Data Volume per Event” from First Principles**

At its core, a *data event* is an atomic occurrence that carries information we wish to persist or process—think of a sensor tick, a user click, or a transaction record.  
The volume \(V\) generated by such an event can be expressed as

\[
V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (C_i + S_i)
\]

where each field \(i\) contributes two parts:

| Component | Meaning | Typical size |
|-----------|---------|--------------|
| **\(C_i\)** | *Fixed* overhead (record header, schema tags, timestamps) | 20–50 bytes |
| **\(S_i\)** | *Variable* payload (raw data, serialized JSON/Avro, binary blobs) | depends on content |

**Why the sum?**  
- **Additivity**: Each field is independent; the total is simply the accumulation of its parts.  
- **Composability**: If a schema evolves (add/remove fields), \(V\) changes linearly with the number of affected fields, making cost forecasting tractable.

The deeper principle at play is *information entropy*: each byte of payload carries a certain amount of Shannon information; the fixed overhead encodes meta‑information that enables downstream systems to interpret those bits. Therefore, optimizing storage or transmission boils down to minimizing both \(C_i\) (e.g., using binary formats like Parquet) and \(S_i\) (compressing high‑entropy data).

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
Even if a payload is highly compressible, the *fixed* overhead can dominate in high‑throughput scenarios. For instance, 10 kB of raw JSON may shrink to 2 kB after compression, but the 30 byte header remains unchanged. Thus, when designing event schemas for streaming platforms, **first reduce field count and use compact encodings; then apply compression**—otherwise you waste bandwidth on what is essentially “metadata.”
