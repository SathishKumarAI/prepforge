Imagine you’re in a kitchen with several chefs and a big pot of soup. **Concurrency** is when the chefs take turns adding ingredients: one stirs while another chops, then they switch. The pot never actually cooks two things at once; it’s just that the tasks overlap in time, giving the illusion of speed.

**Parallelism** is like having separate pots on a multi‑burner stove. Each chef works on their own pot simultaneously, so several soups finish at the same moment. In computing, concurrency lets a single processor handle many tasks by quickly jumping between them; parallelism uses multiple processors to work on different parts of a job at the exact same instant.

So, concurrency is “many things happening in an interleaved way,” while parallelism is “many things truly happening together.”
