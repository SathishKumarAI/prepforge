### Why Reference‑Work Credits Matter

When an evaluation system (e.g., **Langwatch** or **Langfuse**) asks a language model to answer a question, it must judge *how much of the answer comes from the prompt itself* versus *new knowledge generated by the model*.  
Without this split, we cannot tell whether a high score reflects true reasoning or merely parroting back the supplied reference.  

### The Core Principle

Treat every evaluation as an **information‑theoretic channel**:

1. **Input channel**: the prompt + any attached references (the *reference work*).  
2. **Output channel**: the model’s answer.

The mutual information between input and output is maximized when the model simply echoes the reference. To reward genuine inference, we penalise this mutual information by measuring the *surprise* of the output relative to the reference.  

### How Credits Are Computed

1. Tokenise both reference and response.  
2. Compute a *token‑overlap score* (e.g., Jaccard).  
3. Convert overlap into **credits**:  
   \[
   C = 1 - \frac{\text{# overlapping tokens}}{\text{total tokens in response}}
   \]
4. The final evaluation metric is the product of task accuracy and \(C\).

This ensures that a perfect answer with no new content receives zero credit, while an answer that builds on but extends the reference gains full credit.

### Non‑obvious Insight

**Contextual redundancy isn’t just noise; it’s a *prior* for the model.**  
When references are highly redundant, the model learns to treat them as a strong prior and may over‑fit to their wording. By explicitly quantifying and penalising overlap, we implicitly regularise the model against this bias, encouraging *true generalisation* rather than rote memorisation.
