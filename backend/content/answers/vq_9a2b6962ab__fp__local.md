**Why do we regret an expensive purchase?**

At its core, the dilemma is a *trade‑off between present utility and future certainty*.  
When you decide to spend \(C\) dollars on item \(X\), you compare the *expected* pleasure \(\mathbb{E}[U(X)]\) with the *certainty* of saving that money for other uses. In a perfectly rational world, the net benefit \(V=\mathbb{E}[U(X)]-C\) would guide your choice; if \(V<0\), you would never buy.

In reality, we are *loss‑averse*: the pain from an unspent dollar (the “potential loss”) outweighs the pleasure of a purchased good by roughly twice. This asymmetry is captured in prospect theory’s value function \(v(\Delta)\) where \(\Delta = U(X)-C\). Because regret surfaces only after you compare your actual state to the *counterfactual* of not buying, it emerges late: you feel loss‑aversion and hindsight bias simultaneously.

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
Regret is amplified when the purchase *reduces future choice opportunities*. An expensive laptop that forces you into a specific software ecosystem locks you out of cheaper alternatives. The regret signal therefore reflects not just monetary loss but *information loss*—the foregone ability to evaluate other options. Recognizing this, we can mitigate regret by keeping an open “option‑portfolio”: keep the money liquid or buy modular products that preserve future choice flexibility.
