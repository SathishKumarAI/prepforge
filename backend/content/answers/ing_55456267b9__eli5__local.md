Think of your brain’s long‑term memory like a huge, well‑organized library that stores all the facts you’ve learned—words, dates, meanings, and general knowledge about the world. In this “library,” each book is a *semantic memory*: a piece of information that isn’t tied to a particular moment or experience but simply exists as a fact (e.g., “Paris is the capital of France” or “a heart has four chambers”). Just as a librarian can pull any book from the shelf whenever you ask, your brain can retrieve these facts instantly when needed. This fact store is separate from *episodic memory*, which keeps track of personal events like “my birthday party last year.” Semantic memory lets us understand language, solve problems, and learn new things because it holds the building blocks—definitions, rules, and general truths—that we draw upon every day.
