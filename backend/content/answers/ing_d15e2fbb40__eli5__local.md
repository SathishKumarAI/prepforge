Imagine a neighborhood where every house has its own tiny library, and anyone can borrow books directly from any neighbor—no central librarian needed. That’s a peer‑to‑peer (P2P) network: each computer (“peer”) shares files or resources with others.

**Disadvantages**

1. **Security worries** – Just as a book in a neighbor’s house might be missing or tampered with, data on any peer can be corrupted or maliciously altered because there is no central guard.
2. **Unreliable availability** – If the house you need a book from is empty (the computer is off), the book disappears until someone else hosts it again—like files vanishing when a peer disconnects.
3. **Hard to manage updates** – Updating every neighbor’s library copy is tedious; in P2P, keeping all peers synchronized can be slow and error‑prone.
4. **Legal gray areas** – Sharing copyrighted material without permission is easy, just as neighbors might lend out books they don’t own.

So while a P2P setup feels free and democratic, it trades off safety, consistency, and control for that openness.
