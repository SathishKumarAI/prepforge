**Inlier (from a statistical/robust‑estimation standpoint)**  

An *inlier* is any observation that lies within the support of the dominant probability distribution assumed for the data.  
The fundamental problem it solves is separating signal from contamination: when we model a dataset as drawn from a clean process \(p_{\theta}\) corrupted by arbitrary outliers, we wish to estimate \(\theta\) using only those samples that are actually generated by \(p_{\theta}\).  

Why must an inlier satisfy this “support” condition?  
Because any estimator that aggregates data (mean, least‑squares, M‑estimator) is a functional of the empirical distribution. If a point falls outside the support of \(p_{\theta}\), its likelihood under the model is zero; including it would bias the estimator or make it undefined. Thus the inlier set is exactly the intersection of the data space with the manifold where the likelihood is non‑zero.

This concept links to **information geometry**: the clean distribution defines a statistical manifold, and inliers are points whose coordinates lie on that manifold. Outliers correspond to deviations into other manifolds—often due to different generative processes or measurement errors.  

*Non‑obvious insight:*  
An inlier is not merely “not an outlier”; it is *informationally compatible* with the model’s geometry. A point may be close in Euclidean distance yet lie on a different manifold (e.g., a corner of a convex hull that belongs to another cluster). Robust algorithms exploit this by evaluating local likelihood or support rather than raw distance, enabling consistent parameter recovery even when outliers are arbitrarily distributed.
