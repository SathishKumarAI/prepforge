Imagine a rumor that spreads like a bad meme in a crowded classroom: someone whispers it in the hallway, another student repeats it at lunch, and before you know it everyone believes it. That’s *disinformation*—false or misleading information presented as fact to influence opinions or actions.

It thrives because the internet lets anyone post instantly, and people often share stories that feel true or confirm what they already think (confirmation bias). Algorithms in social‑media “recommendations” boost content that gets clicks, so sensational falsehoods spread faster than careful facts. The cost of correcting a rumor is high; by the time evidence arrives, many have already acted on it.

Regulators suggest three tools:  
1. **Transparency**—require platforms to show where stories come from and who paid for them.  
2. **Accountability**—penalize repeat offenders or allow civil lawsuits if people are harmed.  
3. **Education**—fund programs that teach users how to spot fake news, like a digital “fact‑check” filter in their browser.

These measures aim to keep the classroom of ideas honest without silencing real voices.
