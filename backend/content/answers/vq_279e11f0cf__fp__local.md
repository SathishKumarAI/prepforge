**Why the choice of medium matters**

At its core, reading is a *visual‑cognitive mapping* problem: we convert photons into symbols, then integrate them into semantic structures. The physical form of the text—paper vs. screen—affects this mapping at three levels:

1. **Optical fidelity** – Paper offers a matte, diffused surface that reduces glare and preserves the angular distribution of light. This allows the eye to track words with minimal accommodation fatigue. Screens emit directed light; each pixel’s intensity must be calibrated to avoid “screen‑burn” or excessive contrast, which can force the pupil to constrict and increase vergence effort.

2. **Spatial context** – Paper provides a tangible boundary: you know exactly where the page ends. On a screen, especially in scrolling formats, that boundary disappears; the brain struggles to chunk information into discrete units, leading to *semantic spill‑over*—the tendency to carry over context from one section to another and lose focus.

3. **Retention mechanics** – The act of turning a page creates a motor cue that the hippocampus uses as an anchor for episodic memory. This “page‑turn ritual” can boost recall by adding a multimodal signal (visual + proprioceptive). Screens lack this cue unless artificial navigation cues are added, which often feel contrived.

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
The *entropy* of the reading surface matters. Paper’s irregularities introduce low‑frequency noise that paradoxically aids in distinguishing word boundaries and reduces visual crowding. A perfectly uniform screen can make adjacent letters merge perceptually (the “screen‑crowding effect”), especially at higher font densities, which is why many people experience eye strain after prolonged digital reading.

So the preference hinges on how each medium supports or hinders these three processes—optical clarity, spatial segmentation, and mnemonic cueing.
