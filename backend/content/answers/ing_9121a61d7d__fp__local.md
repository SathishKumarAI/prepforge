### 1. The signaling game  
The interview is a two‑player Bayesian game: the candidate (player A) has private information about his *behavioral traits*; the interviewer (player B) wants to estimate that trait from observable actions (answers, questions asked).  
- **Signal**: How you structure your explanation, how you react to “what if” scenarios.  
- **Cost**: Mis‑signal → wasted time; over‑signal → appear arrogant.  

The equilibrium is a *minimal but informative* set of cues that maximizes the interviewer’s posterior belief while keeping cost low.

### 2. Information theory perspective  
Each utterance \(u\) has an information content \(I(u)= -\log P(\text{trait}|\!u)\). The interviewee should choose utterances with high *mutual information* per unit time, e.g., “Tell me about a challenge you solved” → reveals problem‑solving and resilience.  
- **Non‑obvious insight**: It’s not the total length of your story that matters but the *entropy reduction* it delivers; concise, focused anecdotes are more valuable than long narratives.

### 3. Geometry of behavior space  
Map traits (collaboration, ownership, curiosity) to a Euclidean space. The interview is a projection onto observable axes (tone, eye contact). An optimal strategy minimizes the **projection error**: align your projected vector with the interviewer’s target subspace.  
- Example: If the team values *ownership*, emphasize self‑initiated projects; if they value *teamwork*, highlight joint successes.
