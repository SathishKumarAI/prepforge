Imagine a gardener (your computer) trying to grow the perfect tomato plant.  
At first, the gardener has no idea which soil, light, or water schedule works best.  
So the gardener plants many tomatoes, watches how each one grows, and records the conditions that led to good fruit.

In machine learning, the **algorithm** is like that gardener. It starts with a *model*—a simple rule (e.g., “if temperature > 20 °C, then plant A”).  
The **data** are the observations of many plants under different conditions. The algorithm repeatedly checks its rule against these data and adjusts it to reduce mistakes—just as the gardener tweaks soil or watering.

When the model has learned from enough examples, it can predict new outcomes: “Given this weather and soil, will tomato B thrive?” That is the power of machine learning—learning patterns from past experience so you don’t have to guess next time.
