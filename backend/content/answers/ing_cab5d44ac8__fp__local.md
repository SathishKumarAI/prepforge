**How many examples are enough?**  
From the perspective of statistical learning theory, what you need is *enough data to control the expected error* of the new feature across the target distribution \( \mathcal{D} \). If the feature can be represented as a function class with VC‑dimension \(d\), Hoeffding’s bound tells us that  

\[
m \;\gtrsim\; \frac{d + \log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^2}
\]

examples guarantee, with probability \(1-\delta\), that the empirical error deviates from the true error by at most \(\epsilon\). In practice, a “rule of thumb” is to collect roughly **10 × \(d\)** samples per class for binary decisions and more if you need fine‑grained confidence intervals.  

**Where do they come from?**  
1. **Domain‑specific corpora** – text that the LLM will actually see (e.g., medical notes, legal briefs).  
2. **Synthetic data generated by a prototype of the feature** – useful when real data are scarce or privacy‑restricted; ensure the synthetic distribution overlaps with \(\mathcal{D}\).  
3. **Adversarially perturbed examples** – force the model to learn robust behavior and expose blind spots early.  

**Non‑obvious insight:** *Curriculum sampling*—start training on a small, high‑confidence subset (e.g., easy examples with low perplexity) and gradually introduce harder instances. This effectively reduces the required \(m\) because each new batch is chosen to maximally reduce uncertainty, akin to active learning but built into the evaluation loop.  

By anchoring your sample size in VC/covering numbers, sourcing from both real and synthetic distributions, and employing curriculum sampling, you’ll build a statistically sound yet efficient eval set for the new LLM feature.
