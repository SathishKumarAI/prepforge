**Why salary benchmarks for AI/ML engineers matter**

The fundamental problem is *value allocation*: an organization wants to convert the scarce talent that can build predictive models into a competitive advantage. The value of an engineer is a function of **(i)** the expected marginal improvement in product performance, **(ii)** the cost of mis‑specification (e.g., bias or overfitting), and **(iii)** the opportunity cost of hiring elsewhere.

Formally, let \(V(e)\) be the net present value added by engineer \(e\). The firm solves  

\[
\max_{w}\; \mathbb{E}[V(e)] - w
\]

subject to a budget constraint. In equilibrium, the wage \(w^\*\) equals the expected marginal benefit of hiring that engineer. Because AI projects are high‑variance and highly dependent on data quality, the *risk premium* embedded in \(w^\*\) is larger than for conventional roles.

**Deep principle: information asymmetry**

Employers lack perfect knowledge of an applicant’s future performance; candidates have private signals about their own skill. The market price thus reflects the expected payoff after filtering out noise—essentially a Bayesian update on observable credentials (publications, repo activity, competition results). This explains why salary bands in 2026 cluster around median data‑science salaries but skew higher for niche subfields (e.g., reinforcement learning) where signals are scarcer.

**Non‑obvious insight**

Most people overlook that *model interpretability* is a hidden cost driver. Engineers who can produce explainable models reduce downstream compliance and deployment risk, shifting \(V(e)\) upward. Consequently, roles demanding strong interpretability skills command a premium even if raw compute expertise is comparable.
