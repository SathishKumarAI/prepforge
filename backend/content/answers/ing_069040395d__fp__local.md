### Why “Workflow Entry Points” matter

In a machine‑learning pipeline the **data flow** is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of transformations: raw data → preprocessing → feature engineering → model training → evaluation → deployment.  
Each vertex in this DAG can be started independently only if it has *no incoming edges* or all its predecessors have already produced stable artefacts. Those vertices are the **workflow entry points**.

From an optimization viewpoint, entry points define the *minimal cut* of the graph that separates external inputs from internal state. If a node were not an entry point, any change upstream would force recomputation downstream, increasing latency and resource usage. By exposing only those nodes that truly depend on fresh input, we can parallelize execution, cache intermediate results, and apply incremental training without touching unrelated parts of the pipeline.

### A deeper principle

Think of each node as a **function** \(f_i\) with a signature \((X_i)\to Y_i\). The entire workflow is a composition  
\[
F = f_n\circ f_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_1 .
\]
Entry points are exactly the minimal set of functions whose inputs cannot be derived from any other \(f_j\). In category‑theoretic terms, they are the *generators* of the free monoid generated by the DAG. This guarantees that the workflow is **well‑typed**: every data artifact has a unique origin.

### Non‑obvious insight

Most people assume entry points are only “raw‑data” sources. In fact, any **immutable configuration** or **seed model checkpoint** can be an entry point. Treating these as first‑class inputs allows the same workflow engine to switch between different hyper‑parameter regimes or pretrained models without rewriting the graph—essential for reproducible research and A/B testing.

*In short*: Workflow entry points are the minimal, immutable roots of a DAG that enable modularity, parallelism, and reproducibility in ML pipelines.
