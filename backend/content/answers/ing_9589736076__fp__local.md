**Why a “synthesis prompt” is essential for modern language models**

At its core, a synthesis prompt asks the model to *aggregate* disparate pieces of knowledge into a single coherent output. The underlying problem is that large‑scale neural networks learn a probability distribution \(P(\text{output}\mid\text{input})\) over all tokens seen during training. When the input consists of multiple facts or viewpoints, the model must perform an implicit *information fusion* step: it needs to weigh evidence, resolve conflicts, and preserve salient details.

This requirement maps directly onto **Bayesian inference**. Each fact can be viewed as a likelihood term; the synthesis prompt forces the network to compute a posterior that balances them. Because transformers lack explicit memory of past tokens beyond their attention window, they approximate this integration via learned positional encodings and multi‑head attention. The prompt structure—explicitly signalling “combine” or “contrast”—guides the attention weights toward cross‑fact interactions rather than treating each clause independently.

A non‑obvious insight: *the quality of synthesis hinges on token ordering*. Even though transformers are permutation‑invariant in theory, the learned positional biases mean that earlier facts tend to dominate. Thus, carefully arranging premises can steer the posterior toward desired conclusions—an effect often overlooked when drafting prompts.
