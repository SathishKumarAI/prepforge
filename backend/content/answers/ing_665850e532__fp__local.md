**Why an interview is a *search* problem**

At its core, hiring is an optimization task: a company wants the candidate that maximizes expected future contribution minus risk, given limited information.  
An interviewer can only sample a tiny part of a person’s latent skill set—skills, motivation, cultural fit, and adaptability. Each question is a *probe*; the answer gives a noisy estimate of one or more dimensions of that latent vector. The interview process therefore follows a sequential decision‑making framework:  

1. **Prior** – The recruiter holds beliefs about typical skill distributions in the role.  
2. **Evidence** – Behavioral questions (“Tell me about a time you…”) elicit stories that are statistically correlated with underlying traits (e.g., *g* for general cognitive ability, *Grit*, or *Cultural Fit*).  
3. **Posterior Update** – After each answer, the recruiter updates their belief using Bayes’ rule, weighing consistency with prior expectations and the new evidence’s predictive power.  
4. **Stopping Rule** – The process ends when the posterior variance falls below a threshold: enough confidence to decide or reject.

**Why behavioral questions?**

They generate *structured anecdotes* that reduce ambiguity in human judgment. By anchoring on concrete past behavior, interviewers tap into *predictive validity*: people who acted a certain way under similar conditions are likely to act similarly again. This aligns with the “behavior predicts future performance” principle from industrial‑organizational psychology.

**Non‑obvious insight**

Most people treat behavioral questions as simple storytelling. In reality they’re *statistical experiments* designed to elicit *conditional probability distributions* over latent traits. A well‑crafted question reduces the variance of that distribution far more than a generic “Tell me about yourself” does, because it targets a specific dimension (e.g., conflict resolution) and therefore yields higher signal‑to‑noise in the posterior update.

In short: the interview is a Bayesian search; behavioral questions are engineered probes that sharpen our estimate of a candidate’s latent value.
