Imagine a huge library that wants to lend books to people all over the world. If every reader had to travel back to one central shelf in New York, the trip would take hours and the shelves would get crowded. A better way is to set up small “branch” libraries in many cities. Each branch holds copies of the most popular titles so readers can pick them up quickly, without long waits or traffic jams.

A Content Delivery Network (CDN) works like those branch libraries for websites.  
*Content* is any web data—images, videos, text—that users want to see. A CDN places copies of that content on servers scattered around the globe. When someone clicks a link, their request goes to the nearest server instead of the original “home” server. This reduces travel time (latency), lowers the load on the main site, and keeps users happy even during traffic spikes.

That’s why CDNs are so popular: they make websites faster, more reliable, and able to serve millions of visitors at once without crashing.
