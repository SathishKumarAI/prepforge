**Disaster recovery (DR) is the strategy that guarantees a system can resume operation after an event that disables its primary environment.**  
At its core it solves a *probability‑optimization* problem: we want to minimize expected downtime \(E[T]\) subject to budget and performance constraints. The solution is to duplicate critical workloads on a geographically separate “DR site.”  

The DR site must be **independent in three dimensions**:

1. **Physical independence** – different power, cooling, and network paths so that the same fault does not affect both sites.  
2. **Data independence** – continuous replication (e.g., asynchronous or synchronous) ensures that a local data loss at the primary does not erase history.  
3. **Operational independence** – distinct staff, processes, and automation scripts prevent human error from propagating across sites.

Why this architecture? Because the cost of *perpetual* uptime (a single “no‑downtime” deployment) grows super‑exponentially with reliability requirements; a DR site offers a pragmatic trade‑off: pay for one extra copy to avoid catastrophic loss.  

**Non‑obvious insight:** The *latency* between primary and DR sites is often the limiting factor, not storage capacity or compute power. Even with cheap cloud bandwidth, the round‑trip time can bottleneck recovery speed; thus, choosing a region that balances distance and network quality is crucial. In machine‑learning pipelines, this means selecting a DR site that keeps inference latency within acceptable bounds while still protecting training data integrity.
