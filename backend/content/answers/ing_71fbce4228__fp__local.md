**Why interview stats matter**

A hiring process is a *sequential decision problem*: the firm must decide, at each step, whether to advance a candidate based on noisy signals (interviews, tests).  
The goal is to **maximize expected quality of hires** while keeping cost and time low.  

**From Bayes to “probability of acceptance”**

Let \(X\) be a candidate’s true skill, unknown but drawn from the population distribution \(P(X)\).  
An interview yields an observed score \(S\), generated by a likelihood \(P(S|X)\).  
The *posterior* probability that a candidate will succeed in production is  

\[
P(\text{hire}\mid S)=\frac{P(S|\text{hire})\,P(\text{hire})}{P(S)} .
\]

Hiring committees implicitly estimate this posterior from historical data: the fraction of past interviewees with score \(S\) who became productive hires. That fraction is precisely the **interview acceptance rate**.

**Optimal stopping and the “5‑minute rule”**

If interviewing costs are high, a firm will set a threshold \(\tau\). Candidates with \(S<\tau\) are rejected immediately to save time; those above proceed to deeper rounds.  
The optimal \(\tau\) balances marginal hiring value against interview cost—an instance of the *Gittins index* in multi‑armed bandits.

**Non‑obvious insight: the “law of diminishing returns” on question depth**

Adding more technical questions increases discrimination power only until the signal-to-noise ratio saturates. Beyond that, extra questions add **redundant evidence**, inflating cost without improving posterior quality. Empirically this shows up as a plateau in acceptance‑rate curves after 3–4 coding rounds.

**Key takeaway**

Interview statistics are not vanity metrics; they encode the Bayesian update process underlying hiring decisions. Understanding them through probability and optimization reveals why thresholds exist, why some interview formats fail, and where to cut costs without sacrificing quality.
