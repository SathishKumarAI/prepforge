**Why Machine Learning “must” follow certain guiding principles**

At its core, machine learning is a *probabilistic inference* problem: we observe data \((x_i,y_i)\) generated by an unknown stochastic process \(P(y|x;\theta^\*)\). Our goal is to recover a model that approximates this process for future inputs. Two mathematical facts drive the design of any learning algorithm:

1. **Law of large numbers + concentration inequalities**  
   The empirical risk \(\hat{L}(\theta)=\frac1n\sum \ell(y_i,f_\theta(x_i))\) converges to the true risk \(L(\theta)\). Therefore, minimizing \(\hat{L}\) over a *rich but controlled* hypothesis class \(\mathcal H\) guarantees generalization. This is why **bias–variance trade‑off** and regularization (weight decay, dropout) are essential: they shrink \(|\mathcal H|\) while keeping expressive power.

2. **Optimization as inference**  
   Training a neural network solves a high‑dimensional non‑convex minimization problem. Gradient‑based methods exploit the *smoothness* of the loss surface; stochasticity (mini‑batching, dropout) injects noise that helps escape sharp local minima and approximates Bayesian posterior sampling. Thus **stochastic gradient descent** is not just a heuristic—it is an efficient Monte‑Carlo approximation to the true inference problem.

From these principles emerge Zerodha’s guiding philosophies:

| Principle | Origin | Practical implication |
|-----------|--------|-----------------------|
| **Data‑centricity** | Empirical risk minimization | Prioritize data cleaning and augmentation over architectural tweaks. |
| **Simplicity first** | Occam’s razor + VC‑dimension | Start with shallow models; add depth only when validation error plateaus. |
| **Probabilistic grounding** | Bayesian inference | Use ensembles or MC‑Dropout to quantify uncertainty, not just point estimates. |
| **Reproducibility & transparency** | Information theory (mutual information) | Log hyperparameters, random seeds, and calibration curves; treat models as black boxes only after verifying their predictive entropy matches the data distribution. |

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
Most practitioners tune *learning rates* before regularization because gradients dominate early training dynamics. However, from a probabilistic view, the *step size* controls the *effective temperature* of the Markov chain induced by SGD: too high a rate over‑smooths the posterior (high bias), while too low a rate traps the sampler in local modes (high variance). Thus, learning‑rate schedules should be designed to gradually anneal this temperature, mirroring simulated annealing, rather than merely preventing divergence.
