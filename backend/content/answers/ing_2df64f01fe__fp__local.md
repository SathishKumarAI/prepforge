### Why a finite history is unavoidable

A language model learns to predict the next token from a fixed‑size context window (e.g., 8 k tokens). When a conversation exceeds that window, older turns must be discarded or compressed; otherwise the model receives an ill‑formed prompt and loses coherence. The problem reduces to **information bottleneck**: keep enough *signal* for intent while dropping *noise*.  

### Managing the history

| Strategy | How it works | Why it satisfies the bottleneck |
|----------|--------------|---------------------------------|
| **Sliding window** | Keep only the last N tokens (or turns). | Simple, guarantees fixed size. |
| **Hierarchical summarization** | Compress older turns into a short summary that is appended to the prompt. Summaries are generated by a smaller model or rule‑based extraction. | Preserves global intent while freeing space for new content. |
| **Retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG)** | Store all turns in an external vector store; at each turn retrieve the most relevant chunks via semantic similarity and concatenate them to the prompt. | Only the *most informative* past material is fed, reducing redundancy. |
| **Dynamic truncation** | Measure semantic distance between candidate older turns and the current context; discard those with low relevance scores. | Keeps only turns that actually influence the next reply. |

### Non‑obvious insight

Most people treat summarization as a *static* step (e.g., one pass over the conversation). In practice, **iterative refinement**—re‑summarizing after each new turn and re‑ranking relevance—is essential. Each added turn can change which earlier parts are truly needed; a fixed summary quickly becomes stale. By recomputing relevance scores in real time, the system adapts its memory to the evolving dialogue, maintaining coherence without manual tuning.
