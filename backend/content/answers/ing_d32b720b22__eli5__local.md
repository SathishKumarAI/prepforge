Imagine you’re a chef learning to make two kinds of dishes from the same set of ingredients: **soup** (Regression) and **salad** (Classification).  
In *regression*, you want to predict how much soup a pot will hold if you add more carrots, onions, or broth. You give the machine data points—each with amounts of ingredients (inputs) and measured volume (output)—and it learns a smooth recipe that tells you “add X grams of carrot, Y grams of onion, Z ml of broth, and you’ll get about V liters.”  
In *classification*, you’re deciding whether a dish is soup or salad. The machine sees many labeled examples (“this is soup,” “this is salad”) and learns to draw a decision boundary—like a line on a map—so that new, unlabeled dishes can be placed into the correct category.  

*Supervised learning* means the computer receives both inputs (ingredients) and desired outputs (volume or label). *Regression* predicts continuous numbers; *classification* assigns items to discrete groups. This Coursera course teaches how to build those recipes step by step.
