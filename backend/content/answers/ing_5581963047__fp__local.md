### Why a **latency budget** (especially the *P95* value) matters

When you design a system that serves requests in real time, the *user experience* is bounded by the **tail of the latency distribution**, not the mean.  
A single outlier request can freeze an entire UI or violate SLAs, so architects quantify this with a percentile—commonly the 95th (P95).  

**Derivation from first principles**

1. **Goal:** keep *99 %* of responses below a threshold \(T\) that still feels instantaneous to users.  
2. Let \(L\) be the random latency variable. The constraint is \(\Pr(L > T) \le 0.01\).  
3. In practice we estimate this tail from monitoring: compute the empirical 95‑th percentile of recent latencies and compare it to a budget \(B\).  
4. If \(P_{95}(L) > B\), the system is *over‑budgeted*; we must reduce variance (e.g., by caching, better load balancing, or scaling out).

**Deep insight:**  
The P95 budget is not just a safety net—it’s an *optimization objective*. Minimizing variance of \(L\) yields larger gains than reducing mean latency because the tail dominates user perception. Hence, when interviewers ask for your table, they’re probing whether you treat latency as a probabilistic constraint and design with **variance reduction** (e.g., adding redundancy or adaptive routing) in mind, rather than merely targeting average throughput.
