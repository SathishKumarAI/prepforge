### Why we must split data into Train/Dev/Test

At the heart of any predictive model is **generalization**: the ability to perform well on unseen inputs.  
If a model were trained and evaluated on the *same* examples, its loss would be an over‑optimistic estimate; it merely memorizes training labels. To quantify true performance we need a data partition that mimics future distribution.

1. **Training set** – The only data the algorithm sees during learning. Its size balances two competing forces:  
   *More data → lower variance in parameter estimates.*  
   *Too little data → under‑fitting, high bias.*

2. **Development (validation) set** – A held‑out slice used for hyper‑parameter tuning and early stopping. It acts as a “proxy” for unseen data, allowing us to approximate the *expected* loss without touching the test set.

3. **Test set** – Completely untouched until the final evaluation. It provides an unbiased estimate of real‑world performance, guarding against accidental over‑fitting to the dev loop (the *selection bias* problem).

#### Non‑obvious insight  
If the dev and test sets come from different distributions (e.g., temporal drift), the model may appear well‑validated yet fail catastrophically in production. Thus, **distributional alignment**—not just random splitting—is crucial. Techniques like stratified sampling or time‑based splits preserve class proportions or chronological order, ensuring that each partition faithfully mirrors the target distribution.

By rigorously separating data we transform empirical risk minimization into a trustworthy predictor of future behavior, turning statistical estimates into actionable confidence.
