**Why “red‑flag” signals matter**

When an organization hires, it solves a **resource allocation problem**: a finite pool of talent must be matched to tasks that maximize long‑term value. Every candidate presents a *signal*—observable behaviors or statements—that probabilistically indicates their latent skill set and cultural fit. A red flag is simply a signal whose probability distribution skews the expected value downward.

1. **Inconsistent performance** – If past roles show abrupt drops in output, the variance of future productivity rises; higher risk for the employer.  
2. **Poor communication** – Mis‑aligned messaging increases coordination costs, inflating project budgets.  
3. **Unwillingness to learn** – A static skill set contradicts the *learning‑rate* assumption that teams improve over time; a stagnant hire caps growth potential.  

**Common hiring mistakes are the inverse of this reasoning**

| Mistake | Underlying flaw |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Hiring for “culture fit” only** | Treats culture as a static vector; ignores that culture is *dynamic* and that diverse perspectives often expand it, improving innovation (the *Diversity‑Innovation* theorem). |
| **Over‑valuing credentials over experience** | Credentials are cheap signals with low informational value compared to on‑the‑job evidence of problem‑solving. |
| **Failing to test for “growth mindset”** | Neglects the probability that a candidate’s learning curve will flatten, undermining long‑term ROI. |

**Non‑obvious insight:** The *optimal hiring rule* is not “pick the best résumé”; it is to pick the candidate whose **expected marginal contribution**—calculated by weighting observed signals against their predictive power—is highest. This reframes red flags as quantitative risk factors rather than vague intuition, turning hiring into a principled decision‑making process.
