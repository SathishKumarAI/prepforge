Think of the Internet like a giant library that contains millions of books (websites).  
Each book has an easy‑to‑remember title, but inside the library’s catalog you only find its shelf number (the IP address) – a long string of numbers that tells your computer exactly where to go.  
The Domain Name System (DNS) is the “librarian” that translates a friendly name like **www.example.com** into the book’s shelf number. When you type a name, your computer asks the librarian (“What is the IP for www.example.com?”). The librarian looks up its list, finds the correct shelf number, and tells your computer where to fetch the book.  

Without DNS, you’d have to remember every site’s numeric address—just as readers would need to know each shelf number instead of a title. DNS keeps the Internet user‑friendly by mapping memorable names to machine‑readable addresses.
