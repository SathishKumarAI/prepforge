1. **State the Core Problem** – Begin with the *specific* question you’re addressing (e.g., “How can we reduce overfitting in deep neural nets trained on scarce data?”).  
2. **Explain Why It Matters** – Connect the problem to a broader consequence: higher model reliability, safer deployment, or economic impact.  
3. **Highlight Existing Gaps** – Summarize why current solutions fall short (e.g., reliance on hand‑crafted regularizers, lack of theoretical guarantees).  
4. **Introduce Your Approach** – Present your method as a principled response that aligns with a deeper principle—often an optimization or information‑theoretic one. For instance: “We formulate training as minimizing a divergence‑based risk bound, guaranteeing tighter generalization.”  
5. **Emphasize Novelty & Impact** – Conclude by hinting at the key contribution and its implications (e.g., “This yields state‑of‑the‑art accuracy on benchmark datasets while requiring fewer hyperparameters”).  

*Non‑obvious insight*: A well‑written intro doesn’t merely list results; it *derives* why your method must work. By grounding the narrative in a universal principle—such as minimizing an upper bound on expected loss—you invite readers to see the approach as inevitable, not arbitrary. This alignment between motivation and theory is what turns a good introduction into a compelling one.
