Imagine a chef learning to cook by watching countless videos of dishes being made. Each video shows the chef’s hands moving, ingredients mixing, and the final plate tasting. After thousands of hours, the chef can guess the right amount of spice or timing just from looking at an empty pan—without needing a recipe book.

Deep learning is like that chef, but for computers. It uses layered “neural networks” (think of them as layers of decision‑making filters) that automatically learn patterns from huge data sets—images, sounds, text. The deeper the network, the more abstract the features it can detect, from simple edges to complex objects or emotions.

It matters because it lets machines do tasks once thought uniquely human: recognizing faces in photos, translating languages on the fly, driving cars, diagnosing diseases, and even creating art.
