### Why AI‑usage guidelines matter

When interviewing, you’re not just assessing *skills*—you’re evaluating *processes*. If a candidate relies on an LLM to draft code or write essays during the test, you’re measuring how well they **integrate external intelligence** rather than their own reasoning. The fundamental problem is that modern LLMs can produce plausible but incorrect solutions; if candidates let them do all the heavy lifting, you lose insight into their true cognitive workflow.

### How a principled policy works

1. **Define the “source” boundary**:  
   *Allowed*: internal notes, scratch‑pad calculations, open‑book references (official docs).  
   *Disallowed*: any output that is not traceable to the candidate’s own writing or reasoning chain.

2. **Enforce a transparent audit trail**:  
   Every AI‑generated snippet must be annotated with timestamp, prompt, and model version. This turns the “black box” into a reproducible artifact you can review later.

3. **Tie it back to learning objectives**:  
   If the task is algorithm design, ask the candidate to *explain* why each line of code works—an AI can’t generate an explanation that reflects their personal understanding unless they craft it themselves.

### Non‑obvious insight

Most interviewers think “no AI” equals “pure skill.” In reality, a hybrid approach—allowing limited AI usage but requiring explicit justification—often reveals *better* talent. Candidates who can harness AI responsibly demonstrate metacognition: they know when to delegate and how to validate external outputs. This mirrors the real‑world workflow of software engineers who routinely use code assistants but still must own the final product.
