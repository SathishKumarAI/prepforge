Imagine writing a recipe that needs ingredients you can hand‑to‑hand to your kitchen robot. In Python those “ingredients” are **literals** – fixed values written directly in the code.

| Literal type | What it looks like | Analogy |
|--------------|-------------------|---------|
| **Number literals** | `42`, `3.14` | A specific amount of flour or sugar you measure out. |
| **String literals** | `"hello"`, `'Python'` | The exact label on a jar – the text is part of the ingredient itself. |
| **Boolean literals** | `True`, `False` | A tiny flag that says “yes” or “no.” |
| **None literal** | `None` | An empty cup, representing “nothing here.” |

When you type one of these into your program, Python takes it exactly as you wrote it—just like the robot takes a measured cup of flour. These are the only ways to give the language an unchangeable value; everything else (variables, expressions) is built from them.
