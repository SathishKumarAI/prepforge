**Why we care about gift‑giving vs. gift‑receiving**

The core problem is *social coordination*: humans must signal intentions, forge alliances, and maintain reciprocity without explicit contracts. A gift functions as a **cheap talk** that can be verified only by its impact on the receiver’s welfare. The cost of giving (time, money, effort) serves as a credibility signal; the benefit to the giver is reputational capital, which is non‑monetary but valuable in future interactions.

**Derivation**

1. **Information asymmetry**: The giver knows their own motives; the receiver cannot directly observe them. By incurring cost, the giver commits to generosity, reducing uncertainty.
2. **Reciprocity equilibrium**: In a repeated game framework, giving establishes a *tit‑for‑tat* baseline—if I give you something now, you’re more likely to reciprocate later. This lowers future transaction costs for both parties.
3. **Signal of identity and status**: High‑cost gifts signal wealth or altruism; low‑cost, thoughtful gifts signal intimacy. The optimal strategy depends on the social context (e.g., close friends vs. acquaintances).

**Non‑obvious insight**

Most people overestimate how much they enjoy receiving because *gift‑receipt is an instant reward*, while giving requires delayed gratification. Empirical studies show that **the act of choosing and preparing a gift produces more lasting positive affect than simply opening one**, because it activates the brain’s dopaminergic pathways associated with planning and agency.
