A Solid‑State Drive (SSD) is a storage device that keeps your data on tiny memory chips called NAND flash instead of spinning disks.  
Think of it like a super‑fast library: the books are stored in a neat row of shelves (the flash cells), and you can pull any book out instantly because there’s no need to spin or move parts around. In a hard drive, a metal platter spins and a tiny arm slides to read a page—slow, noisy, and prone to wear. An SSD has no moving parts; it uses transistors that remember whether they’re “on” (1) or “off” (0). When you ask for data, the controller sends a quick electrical pulse to the right cells, retrieving information in milliseconds. That’s why SSDs feel instant and are more durable than spinning disks.
