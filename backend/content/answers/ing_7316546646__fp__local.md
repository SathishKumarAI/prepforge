### Availability in Numbers – why it *must* be expressed quantitatively

When architects talk about “high‑availability” they’re not just bragging; they’re solving the **probability of service interruption** problem.  
A system’s uptime is a function of three independent factors:

| Factor | What it captures | How it contributes to availability |
|--------|-----------------|------------------------------------|
| **Redundancy** | Number of active replicas (N) and fail‑over paths (M) | \(P_{\text{up}} = 1 - P_{\text{down}}\) where \(P_{\text{down}} = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_i\) |
| **Mean Time To Repair (MTTR)** | Average time to restore a failed component | Availability ≈ MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) |
| **Deployment strategy** | Rolling vs. blue‑green vs. canary | Reduces simultaneous failure probability, effectively increasing M |

The classic *“four nines”* (99.99 %) is just shorthand for a target uptime of 0.9999, which translates to roughly **5.26 minutes of downtime per year**. If MTTR is 10 minutes, the system can only tolerate ~4 failures per year to stay within that bound—forcing a design that either improves MTTR or adds more redundancy.

#### Non‑obvious insight
Most designers stop at “add replicas.” The *real* lever is **redundancy in *different failure domains***. A 3‑node cluster on the same rack shares power, cooling, and network, so its effective \(p_i\) is higher than a geographically spread set. By quantifying each domain’s failure probability and then multiplying across independent paths, you can predict that a “4‑nines” target may actually require **six** replicas in a single data center plus one in a remote site—far more than intuition suggests.

In short, availability is a *probabilistic budget* that must be allocated between redundancy, repair speed, and deployment strategy. Quantifying each component lets you trade off cost against risk with mathematical certainty rather than gut feeling.
