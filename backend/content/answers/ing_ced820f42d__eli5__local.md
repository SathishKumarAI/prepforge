Think of machine learning like a gardener who wants to grow the best tomato plant possible. The *mutations* are tiny changes the gardener makes—adding a pinch more fertilizer, moving the pot a little farther from the sun, or trimming one leaf. Each change is tested: does the plant grow taller? do its fruits taste sweeter? In machine learning we do the same, but instead of plants we have models (the “plants”) and instead of soil we have data. A *mutation* is any small tweak to a model’s parameters or structure—like nudging one weight up by 0.01 or swapping two hidden layers. We then evaluate the model on new data (like tasting a tomato). If the mutation improves performance, we keep it; otherwise we discard it and try another. This process of trial‑and‑error, guided by measurable results, is how models learn to make better predictions over time.
