Imagine you’re in a kitchen trying to bake a cake. A **single‑agent** (one chef) can handle the whole recipe: mixing, baking, decorating—all at once. It works fine when the task is simple or the chef is very skilled.  

A **multi‑agent** setup is like having several chefs each with a special skill—one chops vegetables, another kneads dough, another handles the oven, and someone else does the final glaze. When the cake requires many steps that can happen in parallel, the whole job finishes faster and often better because each chef focuses on what they do best.  

But if the recipe is very simple or the chefs don’t coordinate well—say one starts frosting before the other has baked—the kitchen gets chaotic. Too many cooks can over‑talk, waste ingredients, and end up with a messy cake.  

So, multi‑agent systems win when tasks are complex, parallelizable, and the agents can share information smoothly. They hurt performance when the task is simple, or coordination overhead outweighs the benefit of division of labor.
