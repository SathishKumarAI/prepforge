Imagine a city of tiny traffic lights that decide how cars move through an intersection. Each light (a “neuron”) looks at the cars in front of it, follows a simple rule—if more than a certain number of cars are waiting, turn green; otherwise stay red—and passes its decision on to the next lights. When many such lights work together, they can learn to handle complex traffic patterns: sometimes they open early for rush hour, other times they hold back during quiet streets.

A neural network is just that city made up of layers of artificial “traffic lights.” Each neuron receives numbers (like car counts), applies a small rule, and sends its result forward. By tweaking the rules—called weights—in thousands or millions of neurons, the network learns to solve tasks such as recognizing faces or translating language, all without us writing explicit instructions.
