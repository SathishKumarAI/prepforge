**Why AI spreads fast yet stays in big firms**

At its core, adopting an AI system is a *resource‑allocation* problem: each firm must decide how much capital to spend on data, compute, talent, and integration versus the expected return. The benefit curve is highly convex—early investments yield modest gains, but once a critical mass of labeled data or model performance is reached, returns explode (the “learning‑curve” effect). Large firms already possess the *critical mass* of both data and cash; their marginal cost of scaling AI is low relative to small firms.  

The speed of diffusion follows from two intertwined dynamics:

1. **Knowledge spillover** – As big players publish models, patents, or open‑source code, the *barrier to entry* drops for everyone.  
2. **Network effects** – AI tools that integrate with existing enterprise platforms (ERP, CRM) become more valuable as more users adopt them, creating a self‑reinforcing loop.

Yet, small firms face *diminishing marginal returns*: they can’t afford the same data volume or compute budget, and their integration costs are disproportionately high. Thus, while adoption accelerates globally, the *rate of return* remains skewed toward those with deep pockets.

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
The concentration is not merely a “big‑company advantage” but an *information asymmetry* issue. Large firms can afford to experiment and absorb failures; their failure cost is spread across many projects, making them risk‑tolerant. Small firms, in contrast, cannot tolerate costly missteps, so they adopt only proven solutions—often those already optimized by the giants—reinforcing the concentration loop.
