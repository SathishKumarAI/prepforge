Imagine training a puppy to fetch a ball. The puppy (the “agent”) doesn’t know the best way to get the ball, so it tries many actions: sit, jump, run, bark. Every time it succeeds in bringing the ball back, its owner gives it a treat (a positive reward). If it gets lost or knocks over a vase, it receives a gentle reprimand (negative reward). Over time, the puppy learns which behaviors lead to more treats and avoids those that bring punishment.  

In reinforcement learning, an algorithm behaves like that puppy: it interacts with an environment, tries different actions, and receives feedback in the form of rewards or penalties. It keeps adjusting its strategy so that future actions maximize cumulative reward—just as a well‑trained dog learns to fetch faster and smarter. The key terms are “agent” (the learner), “environment” (the world it acts in), “action” (what it does), and “reward” (feedback signal).
