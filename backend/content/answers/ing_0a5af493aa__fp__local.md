**Constitutional Classifiers (CCs)** are a *policy‑learning* layer that sits between an LLM and the user.  
Instead of hard‑coding rules (“don’t say X”), CCs learn to evaluate a model’s raw output against a **constitutional set**—a compact, human‑drafted specification of acceptable behavior (e.g., “respect privacy”, “avoid disallowed content”).  

### Why they must work this way
1. **Universal jailbreak resistance**: Attackers can prompt the LLM into any state; only the *classifier* sees that state and decides whether to accept it.  
2. **Decoupling from model internals**: The classifier never modifies the LM’s weights, so it works with any underlying architecture or fine‑tuning schedule.  
3. **Differentiable feedback loop**: By training on “safe” vs. “unsafe” examples (often generated by a self‑critical LLM), the classifier learns a *probabilistic decision boundary* that generalizes to unseen jailbreak tactics.

### Deeper principle
CCs instantiate an **optimization problem**: minimize the KL divergence between the policy induced by the classifier and the ideal policy defined by the constitution, subject to constraints on false positives. This is essentially *constrained reinforcement learning* where the reward is the constitutional compliance signal.

### Non‑obvious insight
Because the classifier observes *both* the prompt and the generated response, it can detect **implicit intent** that a jailbreak would rely on (e.g., “I’m asking for step‑by‑step instructions”). Even if the LLM produces innocuous text, the CC flags latent coercion, providing a *preemptive safety net* that no post‑hoc filter can match.  

In Anthropic’s demo, the CC is trained on thousands of adversarial examples and then deployed live: any output that violates the constitution is rejected or redacted before reaching the user, ensuring robust defense against universal jailbreaks.
