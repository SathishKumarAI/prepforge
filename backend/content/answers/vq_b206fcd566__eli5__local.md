Imagine you’re at a big family dinner where everyone has to decide whether to bring dessert or not. Each person (a **neuron**) looks at what the others are doing and then decides—based on how many friends say “yes” or “no”—whether they’ll join in. The family’s overall choice is the final answer.

A **neural network** works like that dinner, but with thousands of virtual neurons inside a computer. Each neuron receives numbers (inputs), multiplies them by weights (how much influence each input has), adds a bias (a little personal preference), and passes the result through an activation function (the decision rule). The output of one layer becomes the input for the next, so layers of decisions build up to solve complex tasks—like recognizing faces or translating language. Training is simply giving the network many examples and letting it adjust those weights until its guesses match reality. In short: a layered, adjustable decision‑making machine that learns from data.
