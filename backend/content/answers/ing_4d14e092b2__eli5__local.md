Imagine a chef who learns to cook by tasting dishes instead of following a recipe book. At first the chef just tries random ingredients and notes which combinations make people smile. Over time, she notices patterns: adding salt balances sweetness, chopping onions before cooking releases aroma, and simmering for 30 minutes brings out flavor. Eventually, the chef can predict what will taste good in new dishes without tasting them all over again.

That’s machine learning in a nutshell. Instead of hard‑coding rules, a computer is given lots of examples—data—and it “learns” the hidden patterns that link inputs to outputs. Once trained, it can make predictions or decisions on brand‑new data, just as the chef anticipates flavor profiles without tasting every single dish.

Key terms:  
- **Data** – The examples the system sees (e.g., pictures of cats).  
- **Model** – The internal representation that captures patterns.  
- **Training** – Feeding data to adjust the model’s parameters so it gets better at the task.  

Like a chef refining recipes through practice, machine learning refines its model through experience, turning raw data into useful insights or actions.
