Imagine your brain as a kitchen where you cook dishes (process data). A **cache** is like a small prep table right next to the stove that holds ingredients you use most often—cheese, salt, onions—so you don’t have to walk back to the pantry every time. In computing, an *in‑memory database* plays that role: it keeps frequently accessed data in RAM (the “prep table”) instead of on a hard drive (the pantry). When a program asks for information, the cache checks first; if the data is already there (“on the prep table”), it returns it instantly. If not, it fetches from the slower storage and then places a copy in memory for future use. This keeps your “recipes” running smoothly, much faster than always rummaging through a distant pantry.
