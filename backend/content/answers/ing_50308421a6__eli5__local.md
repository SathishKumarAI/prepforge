Think of a clinician using an AI like a chef tasting a dish before serving it. The **edit rate**—how often the doctor changes the AI’s suggested plan—is like counting how many times the chef tweaks the seasoning. It tells you something: if edits are rare, the chef (clinician) trusts the recipe (AI). But it hides other things. A low edit rate could mean the chef is just following the recipe without questioning it, or that the dish is so bland the chef has no better idea. It also ignores how often the AI gets it right in the first place.

A clearer tool would be a **confidence‑matching dashboard**: show the AI’s own confidence score next to each recommendation and record whether the clinician accepts, modifies, or rejects it. Pair this with a quick “Did the patient do better?” check after the visit. That way you see not just how much editing happens, but whether the edits actually improve outcomes—truly measuring trust in action.
