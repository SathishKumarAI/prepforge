**Why we gravitate toward a particular landscape**

At its core, the question asks how our brains translate environmental cues into affective value. Evolutionarily, early humans who chose habitats that maximized safety, resource availability, and social cohesion survived longer. Each terrain type offers distinct *information* about those variables:

| Terrain | Key signals | Adaptive payoff |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Beach | Open horizon → low predator density; salt spray → hydration cues | Rapid foraging of marine foods, low predation risk |
| Mountain | Steep gradients → abundant game & diverse micro‑climates | High caloric intake from varied flora/fauna |
| Forest | Dense canopy → high cover and moisture | Shelter, food diversity, reduced visibility to predators |

Our **preference** is thus a learned weighting of these signals. The brain’s *default mode network* maps sensory input onto an internal reward landscape; the terrain that maximizes expected utility (energy gain + safety) wins.

### Non‑obvious insight
People often think of “favorite” landscapes as purely aesthetic, but they are really *prospect–refuge* optimizers. The subtle balance between visible openness and hidden cover—what psychologists call **prospicence**—determines the emotional valence we assign to each setting. A beach feels safe because its open view signals few hiding predators; a forest feels secure because its density offers escape routes, even though it may appear riskier at first glance. Thus, our preference is less about color or texture and more about how the terrain encodes survival cues into an internal reward signal.
