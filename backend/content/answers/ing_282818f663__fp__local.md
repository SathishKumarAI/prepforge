In the modern economy a contractor’s *contract* is an *option*: the employer pays a premium to lock in a service that could otherwise be obtained at zero marginal cost from a truly independent market.  
1. **Risk‑sharing** – The firm internalizes the risk of non‑delivery, mis‑pricing or quality failure. If the contractor defaults, the employer bears the loss; if it delivers early, the employer captures upside.  
2. **Information asymmetry** – Firms have better information about their own needs and the contractor’s track record than the market does. Paying for “in‑house” status reduces the transaction cost of monitoring and enforcing quality.  
3. **Economies of scale** – A payroll relationship gives the firm bargaining power: bulk discounts on software, shared infrastructure, or access to a talent pool that would be too costly if each contractor were negotiated separately.  

**Non‑obvious insight:** The “payroll” label is often a *legal fiction* that lets firms claim ownership of intellectual property and control over the contractor’s work without formally hiring them as employees. This hybrid status enables firms to reap the benefits of an employee (control, IP) while avoiding statutory obligations (benefits, overtime). Thus contractors stay on payroll not because they are truly employees, but because the hybrid structure maximizes risk‑adjusted returns under regulatory constraints.
