Imagine you’re at a big birthday party where every guest brings a snack.  
*Joint distribution* is like looking at the whole table of snacks—how many people brought chips, cookies, or fruit together. It tells you the probability of seeing any specific combination (e.g., 3 chip bags and 2 cookie boxes).  

*Marginal distribution* is what you get if you ignore one type of snack and just count another. If you look only at how many people brought chocolate bars regardless of what else they carried, that’s a marginal view—summing over all the other possibilities.  

*Conditional distribution* zooms in on the “if‑then” part: suppose you know someone did bring a fruit basket; now you ask, “Given that fact, what’s the chance they also brought cookies?” You’re conditioning on one event and looking at the remaining probability.  

So joint = whole picture, marginal = single dimension of that picture, conditional = focused on one slice after we fix something else.
