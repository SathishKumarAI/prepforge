Imagine you’re a chef who’s only ever seen pictures of a cake but never baked one. When asked to “make” a cake, you pull out a pre‑baked loaf and call it “cake.” That’s **fake familiarity**: sounding like you know the recipe while actually using a stand‑in that looks similar.

In machine learning interviews this happens when a candidate talks about a model or algorithm as if they’ve built or tuned it, but in reality they’re just reciting textbook definitions. They might say “I used a neural net with dropout” and then describe how dropout works instead of showing code or explaining how they chose the dropout rate for their own data.

Key signs:  
- **Surface‑level explanations** that match a video or article.  
- No concrete examples from personal projects.  
- Repetitive, generic statements (“I trained a model”) without details on dataset, preprocessing, or hyperparameter choices.

To spot it, ask for specifics: “What loss function did you use?” or “Show me the part of your code where you handle class imbalance.” A genuine engineer will point to their own experiments, not just textbook theory.
