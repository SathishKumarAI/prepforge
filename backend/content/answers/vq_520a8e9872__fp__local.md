**Why Sales and Discounts Matter (or Not)**  
At its core, a discount is a *price‑signal* that trades the immediate monetary value for future utility: “I’ll buy now at $X$ instead of waiting for $Y$.” The decision hinges on two competing processes:

1. **Temporal Discounting** – human agents evaluate delayed benefits as less valuable (hyperbolic discounting). A sale exploits this by making the purchase feel *immediate*.
2. **Scarcity & Loss‑Aversion** – limited‑time offers trigger a perceived loss if ignored, magnifying the utility of buying now.

Mathematically, expected value \(EV = p \times U\) where \(p\) is probability of benefit and \(U\) is utility. A discount increases \(p\) (by making purchase easier) while also inflating \(U\) through perceived savings. In a *bounded‑rational* setting, the marginal cost of waiting (time, risk of price rise) outweighs the marginal gain from a higher price later.

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
People often *overestimate* how much they’ll save on a sale, ignoring that the “discount” is merely a psychological lever. The true benefit lies in the *reallocation of attention*: a discount frees cognitive bandwidth for other purchases or leisure, effectively increasing overall consumption utility beyond the monetary saving.

So sales are important **only** if the psychological payoff (attention shift + loss‑aversion) outweighs the actual price differential. If you’re already price‑elastic and rational, discounts add little real value—just a trick of perception.
