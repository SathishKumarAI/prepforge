### Why *sampling* (inference) is the heart of a language model

A language model learns a probability distribution \(p_{\theta}(x_{1:T})\) over token sequences.  
During inference we must **sample** tokens one by one, because the joint probability factors as  

\[
p_{\theta}(x_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T} p_{\theta}(x_t \mid x_{<t}),
\]

so each new token depends only on the past.  
The model’s forward pass gives us a *logits vector* \(z_t = W h_t + b\) for every possible next token, where \(h_t\) is the hidden state after seeing \(x_{<t}\). Converting logits to probabilities via softmax

\[
p_{\theta}(x_t=k \mid x_{<t}) = \frac{\exp(z_t[k]/T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_t[j]/T)}
\]

introduces a **temperature** \(T>0\) that controls randomness:  

* \(T=1\) – native distribution.  
* \(T<1\) – sharper, more deterministic (“greedy”).  
* \(T>1\) – flatter, more creative.

Sampling is thus an *online Monte‑Carlo* approximation of the true sequence distribution; it is also the only way to generate arbitrary-length text because we cannot precompute all future tokens at once.  

### Non‑obvious insight

The *choice of temperature* can be viewed as a **variational trade‑off**: lower \(T\) reduces entropy but increases bias toward high‑probability paths; higher \(T\) adds exploration, which is crucial for long‑term coherence in GPTs trained on limited data. In nanoGPT the simple `sample()` function exposes this trade‑off directly, letting users experiment with how much *uncertainty* they want to inject into each step without altering the underlying model weights.
