Imagine you’re a chef who wants to learn how to cook a new dish just by tasting it over and over. Every time you taste, you note the flavor (the data), adjust your seasoning (the model’s parameters), and try again. After many trials, you discover a recipe that consistently tastes great—this is what machine learning does.

**Machine learning** is a set of methods that let computers learn patterns from data instead of being told step‑by‑step how to act. The **data** are like the tastings: numbers or images that describe something in the world. A **model** is the chef’s evolving recipe—a mathematical function that maps input data (ingredients) to output predictions (flavors). During **training**, the model repeatedly compares its prediction to the true answer, calculates an error, and adjusts itself so future predictions get closer—just as a chef tweaks seasoning.

When the model has learned well, it can take new, unseen data and produce useful outputs—like predicting tomorrow’s weather or recognizing a cat in a photo. That is the core idea of machine learning: learn from examples, then generalize to new situations.
