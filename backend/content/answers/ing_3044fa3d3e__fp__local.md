**Stage 3: On‑Site (4–5 Interviews + Founder Round)**  
The on‑site is the *decision point* where a candidate’s fit is evaluated under the same conditions they will work in. It translates abstract qualifications into observable behaviors by exposing the applicant to every stakeholder who will shape or be shaped by their performance.

1. **Problem framing** – Hiring is a costly experiment: each interview costs time, opportunity cost, and potential loss if the wrong person is hired. The on‑site condenses this experiment into a high‑signal window: five interviews plus a founder conversation provide enough data points to estimate a candidate’s *latent skill vector* (technical depth, cultural fit, learning agility).  
2. **Why 4–5 interviews?**  
   - *Redundancy vs. precision*: With n independent evaluators, the variance of the mean score shrinks as \(1/n\), but diminishing returns set in after ~5 because interviewers’ biases are correlated (same company culture, same product).  
   - *Coverage*: Different lenses—technical, design, product, ops—ensure orthogonal signals.  
3. **Founder round as a Bayesian update** – Founders hold the prior on what success looks like in this startup. Their conversation is essentially a *posterior* recalibration: they ask scenario questions that test the candidate’s alignment with the company’s vision and risk tolerance. The founder’s assessment carries more weight because founders are the ultimate decision makers and their intuition often correlates strongly with long‑term fit, an effect amplified in high‑growth environments where cultural cohesion is paramount.  
4. **Non‑obvious insight**: *The interview sequence matters*. Presenting the most challenging or unfamiliar topic first forces the candidate to surface underlying competence; subsequent interviews then confirm or refute that initial signal rather than merely reinforcing it. This “reverse order” reduces the likelihood of a candidate gaming early interviews and aligns with the principle of **information asymmetry resolution**—the interviewer learns more from the hardest question than from a comfortable one.

In short, Stage 3 compresses a complex, high‑stakes decision into a tightly orchestrated set of observations that maximally reduce uncertainty while respecting the startup’s unique cultural DNA.
