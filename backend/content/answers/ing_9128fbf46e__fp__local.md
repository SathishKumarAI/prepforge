**Why do AI‑engineer salaries vary so wildly?**

At the core of any salary is *information asymmetry*: a firm knows how much an engineer can deliver in terms of model accuracy, inference latency, and maintainability; the worker only knows their own skill set. In equilibrium, pay is set where the marginal value of a new hire’s contribution equals the marginal cost of that hire.  

For AI, the *marginal value* is highly non‑linear:

1. **Data‑driven performance** – A model’s error drops roughly as \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) with data size \(N\). Once a baseline is achieved, extra expertise yields diminishing returns, so early hires are paid more.
2. **System integration** – Deploying an LLM to production involves security, compliance, and scaling; the probability of a costly failure scales super‑linearly with model complexity, inflating pay for engineers who can bridge research and ops.
3. **Opportunity cost of scarcity** – The set of engineers who can simultaneously handle deep learning theory, distributed training, and software engineering is tiny. Their utility function includes not just current projects but the *future* of AI in the firm.

A non‑obvious insight: **salary spikes often align with “lock‑in” points—when a company reaches a threshold where an additional engineer can shift from prototype to product, drastically reducing future costs**. Thus, pay is less about raw skill and more about being at that tipping point in the organization’s AI lifecycle.
