Imagine you’re at a bakery and you see someone wearing a red hat. You want to know the chance that they’re from the “red‑hat” family who always buys croissants (the *hypothesis*). The only evidence you have is the hat color.

Bayes’ theorem says:  
**How likely is the hypothesis, given the evidence?**  

It does this by comparing two things:

1. **Prior belief** – before seeing the hat, how common are red‑hat family members in the bakery (the *prior probability*)?
2. **Likelihood** – if someone *is* from that family, how likely is it they’d wear a red hat (the *likelihood*)?

You combine these with the overall chance of seeing a red hat at all, and you get the updated probability that the person belongs to the red‑hat family.  
So Bayes simply updates your guess when new evidence arrives, much like adjusting a recipe based on taste tests—each bite refines what you think is best next time.
