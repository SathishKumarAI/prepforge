### From the Ground Up

When an AI system is introduced, stakeholders implicitly solve a **resource allocation problem**: they ask “Will this tool deliver X outcome?” The *expected utility* of deploying the AI is compared against the cost of training, integration, and maintenance. If the expectation exceeds what the model can realistically achieve—because of data gaps, algorithmic bias, or mis‑aligned objectives—the system fails, leading to disappointment.

The **behavioural layer** emerges because humans are not purely rational agents; they rely on *heuristics* (availability, representativeness) and are prone to *overoptimism*. An AI’s failure then triggers a cascade: trust erodes, usage drops, and the very knowledge that could improve the model is lost. Thus, managing expectations becomes an optimisation problem of its own—maximising long‑term utility while minimising reputational cost.

**Why it must work this way:**  
1. **Signal‑to‑Noise Ratio** – Early over‑promises drown out genuine performance signals.  
2. **Feedback Loops** – Users who feel misled stop providing the data needed to close the learning loop, stalling improvement.  
3. **Cognitive Dissonance** – People reconcile failure by dismissing the AI, not the underlying issue.

#### One Non‑Obvious Insight
The *real* cost of a failed expectation is not just lost money; it’s the *latent information* that would have been harvested from continued engagement. A modest upfront policy—such as transparent uncertainty reporting or staged feature rollouts—acts like an *information valve*, allowing early failures to be identified without shutting down the entire system, thereby preserving the data ecosystem essential for future learning.
