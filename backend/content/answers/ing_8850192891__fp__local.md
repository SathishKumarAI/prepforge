**Why do we need different attention mechanisms?**

At the heart of any sequence model is the *optimization* problem: learn a mapping \(f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\) that minimizes expected loss \(\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[\ell(f(x),y)]\).  
A naive encoder–decoder computes each output as a function of *all* inputs, incurring quadratic cost and blurring local structure.  Attention restores locality by weighting contributions \(a_{ij}\) that satisfy
\[
\sum_j a_{ij}=1,\quad a_{ij}\ge0,
\]
turning the model into a convex combination of input‑dependent features.  

Different **attention variants** arise when we alter:
| Variant | Core change | Why it matters |
|---------|-------------|----------------|
| *Scaled dot‑product* (Transformer) | \(a_{ij}=\text{softmax}\!\big(\frac{q_i^\top k_j}{\sqrt d}\big)\) | Stabilises gradients when embedding dimension grows. |
| *Additive* (Bahdanau) | \(e_{ij}=v^\top\tanh(W_q q_i+W_k k_j)\) | Works better with low‑dimensional embeddings; fewer parameters. |
| *Sparse* (Sparsemax, Sinkhorn) | Projects onto simplex with zero mass | Encourages interpretable, hard alignments and reduces computation. |
| *Relative* (Transformer‑XL) | Adds position bias to scores | Captures long‑range dependencies without absolute positions. |

**Non‑obvious insight:**  
Attention is not merely a “soft lookup”; it is an *adaptive convex re‑weighting* that turns the encoder output into a **probability distribution over input features**.  Because this distribution lives on a simplex, any change to its geometry (e.g., sparsity or relative bias) directly modifies the *information bottleneck* of the model: we are learning which bits of the input are most informative for each target token.  Thus, selecting an attention variant is tantamount to choosing a different regularizer on that information flow, which explains why seemingly minor score‑normalisation tricks can have outsized effects on convergence and interpretability.
